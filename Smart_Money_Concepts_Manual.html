01STRUCTURE

Market Structure & Trend

The sequence of swing highs and swing lows is the only true definition of trend — everything else in SMC is built on top of it.

In an uptrend, price prints higher highs (HH) and higher lows (HL) — each pullback holds above the prior low before the next leg up. A downtrend mirrors this with lower highs (LH) and lower lows (LL). The moment that sequence breaks is the moment the market is telling you something changed.

A swing point only exists once price has moved back through it — you can't know a high is a high until price has fallen away from it. That confirmation delay is unavoidable, and it's the same reason no structure tool can be both instant and non-repainting.

  • Higher High (HH): a swing high printed above the previous swing high
  • Higher Low (HL): a swing low printed above the previous swing low
  • Lower High / Lower Low: the mirror image, defining a downtrend
  • A trend stays intact until its defining swing point is broken by a close — not just a wick

02STRUCTURE

Break of Structure (BOS)

A BOS is trend continuation — price closes beyond the most recent swing point, in the direction it was already going.

When an uptrend's most recent swing high is taken out by a candle close, that's a bullish BOS: the uptrend has proven itself again. BOS doesn't predict anything new — it confirms the existing trend is still in force and hands you a fresh, real swing point to work with.

Because BOS requires a close beyond the level, not just a wick through it, it's inherently an after-the-fact confirmation. That lag is exactly what makes it reliable rather than noisy.

  • Price closes beyond the prior swing high (bullish) or swing low (bearish)
  • Occurs in the direction of the already-established trend
  • Each BOS resets the “next level to watch” to the newest swing point

03STRUCTURE

Change of Character (CHoCH)

The same break mechanic as BOS — but against the prevailing trend, making it the earliest structural clue of a reversal.

If price is making lower highs and lower lows, then closes back above the most recent lower high, that's a bullish CHoCH — structure has “changed character.” It's the first structural signal of a potential reversal, which also makes it the least confirmed: a CHoCH can fail and the original trend can resume.

This is why CHoCH is treated as a cue to reassess, not an entry signal on its own — professional use pairs it with a zone (an Order Block or FVG left behind by the reversal move) before acting on it.

  • Same break mechanic as BOS, but breaks against the current trend direction
  • First HH after a run of LH/LL (bullish), or first LL after a run of HH/HL (bearish)
  • Frequently preceded by a liquidity sweep of the opposing side

A downtrend's last lower high gets closed through — the first structural evidence of a reversal.

04STRUCTURE

Internal Structure (iBOS / iCHoCH)

The same BOS/CHoCH logic, run on a shorter lookback, tracking the smaller swings inside a larger move.

A macro uptrend doesn't move in a straight line — its pullbacks have their own miniature structure. Watching that internal structure lets you catch continuation earlier (an internal BOS confirming the pullback is over) without waiting for the macro pivot to be retested.

Internal and macro structure can disagree. An internal CHoCH inside a still-intact macro uptrend is usually just a pullback, not a reversal — reading both is how you tell the difference.

  • Identical mechanics to macro structure, run on a tighter lookback (e.g. 3 bars vs. 8)
  • Used to time entries within a macro leg, not to redefine the macro trend itself
  • Internal structure disagreeing with macro = pullback, not reversal, until macro also breaks

05ZONES OF INTEREST

Order Blocks

The last candle of the “wrong” color before an impulsive move that breaks structure — the footprint of the order flow that caused the break.

For a bullish break, look at the candle immediately before the impulsive rally: the last down-close candle. Its range — body or full wick, depending on convention — is the Order Block. The working theory: unfilled institutional orders remain resting in that zone, and when price returns to it, it can act as support the same way it did the first time.

An Order Block is only valid until it's mitigated — the first time price trades back into the zone, its job is considered done, whether or not it actually holds from there.

  • Last opposite-colored candle before the impulsive leg that causes a BOS or CHoCH
  • Bullish OB = last down-close candle before an up-move; Bearish OB = last up-close before a down-move
  • Mitigated the first time price returns and touches the zone

06ZONES OF INTEREST

Breaker Blocks

When an Order Block fails outright — price closes straight through it — it doesn't disappear. It flips polarity and becomes a Breaker.

A bullish Order Block that gets closed through isn't neutral afterward: the same logic that made it support now makes it resistance, because the participants relying on that zone holding got trapped on the wrong side. When price retraces back up into a broken bullish OB from below, that retest is the breaker setup — a short, not a long.

Breakers are one of the higher-conviction setups in SMC precisely because they require a failure first: the zone has already proven it doesn't hold, which is information a fresh, untested Order Block simply doesn't have yet.

  • Starts as a normal Order Block that gets closed through, not just wicked
  • Polarity flips: former demand becomes supply, or former supply becomes demand
  • The trade is the retest of the broken zone — not the initial break itself

07ZONES OF INTEREST

Fair Value Gaps (FVG)

A three-candle imbalance — a price range the market moved through so fast it left no trade behind — and imbalances tend to get revisited.

Compare candle 1's high/low to candle 3's high/low. If candle 3's low sits above candle 1's high (in an up-move), the space between them was never traded through — that gap is the FVG. Price often returns to “fill” it before continuing, and the exact midpoint of the gap — the Consequent Encroachment (CE) — is commonly treated as the more precise reaction level within it.

FVGs are a byproduct of Order Blocks, not a substitute for them. The strongest confluence is an FVG sitting directly inside or beside an Order Block, not an FVG trading alone.

  • Three-candle pattern: a gap between candle 1's high/low and candle 3's low/high
  • CE = the exact 50% midpoint of the gap
  • “Filled” once price trades back completely through the gap

A 3-candle imbalance during an impulsive move — the untraded space is the FVG, its midpoint the CE.

08LIQUIDITY

Liquidity Pools (EQH / EQL)

Retail stop-losses and breakout orders cluster just beyond obvious swing highs and lows — that resting order flow is liquidity, and price is drawn to it.

When two or more swing highs form at nearly the same price, that's not a coincidence to ignore — it's a visible pool of buy-side liquidity (BSL) sitting just above both highs, because everyone who shorted between them has a stop resting in roughly the same place. The mirror logic applies below equal lows for sell-side liquidity (SSL).

Because this liquidity acts like a magnet, equal highs/lows are some of the most reliable levels for anticipating where price is likely to reach — even briefly — before reversing.

  • Two or more swing highs (or lows) within a small tolerance of each other
  • BSL rests just above equal highs; SSL rests just below equal lows
  • Untouched liquidity exerts a pull on price even from a distance

09LIQUIDITY

Liquidity Sweeps

Price wicks through a liquidity pool to trigger the resting orders there, then closes back on the other side — the grab happens, then the market reverses.

This is the mechanical difference between a real breakout and a liquidity grab: a genuine break closes beyond the level; a sweep only wicks beyond it before snapping back. Seeing the wick without the close is one of the cleaner tells that a move was about clearing out orders, not starting a new trend.

Sweeps matter most at the extremes of a range or right before a CHoCH — a sweep of the opposing liquidity is often the exact fuel a reversal needs.

  • Price trades beyond a known high/low (wick) but closes back inside it
  • Higher-probability at range extremes, session highs/lows, and equal highs/lows
  • Often the candle immediately preceding a CHoCH

Price wicks above the prior high — sweeping resting liquidity — then closes back below it and reverses.

10LIQUIDITY

Higher-Timeframe Liquidity

The same liquidity logic scales up — previous day, week, and month highs/lows are reference levels that sit outside your current chart's structure entirely.

A Previous Day High isn't a pattern computed from the candles in front of you — it's simply where price closed the range on a higher timeframe you may not even have open. These levels matter because a large amount of institutional order flow is benchmarked against them, independent of whatever local structure is doing on your entry timeframe.

A level that's been swept once (wicked, not closed through) carries less weight going forward than one still fully intact — the same strong/weak logic that applies to local swing points applies here too, just at a larger scale.

  • PDH/PDL, PWH/PWL, PMH/PML: the previous period's high/low, always from a fully closed period
  • “Strong” = still untouched; “Weak” = already swept once; “Broken” = closed through
  • Most useful as a target or as confluence with local structure, rarely a standalone signal

11RANGE & TIMING

Premium / Discount / Equilibrium

Split a swing range exactly in half: everything above the midpoint is “expensive” (premium), everything below is “cheap” (discount) — relative to that range, not in any absolute sense.

The logic follows directly from Order Blocks and liquidity: you generally want to be buying in discount, where demand zones and swept sell-side liquidity live, and selling in premium, where supply zones and swept buy-side liquidity live — not chasing price into the expensive half of a range where liquidity has already been spent. The exact 50% midpoint is equilibrium: the point of least conviction either way.

This only works if the range it's measured from is the right one. A range that resets on every minor pullback isn't the one institutional flow is actually respecting — see Dealing Range, further down.

  • Premium = upper half of the active range; Discount = lower half
  • Equilibrium = the exact 50% midpoint of the range
  • Favor discount entries in an uptrend, premium entries in a downtrend

One swing range split at its 50% equilibrium — the upper half is premium, the lower half discount.

12RANGE & TIMING

Optimal Trade Entry (OTE)

Within a discount or premium zone, the 62%–79% retracement pocket is treated as the highest-probability entry band — deep enough to align with an Order Block, shallow enough that the move hasn't already failed.

OTE narrows Premium/Discount from “buy anywhere in the cheap half” down to one specific, testable pocket. In practice, this is where an Order Block, an FVG's CE, and a liquidity sweep all tend to cluster together — which is the actual reason it works, not the Fibonacci ratio by itself.

Treat OTE as a zone to build confluence in, not a level to enter on contact. Price reaching the pocket is necessary — it is not sufficient on its own.

  • Retracement zone between the 62% and 79% levels of the active swing
  • Strongest when it overlaps an Order Block or an FVG's CE
  • A pocket to watch for confirmation in, not an automatic trigger

After an impulsive leg, price retraces into the 62–79% pocket before the trend resumes.

13RANGE & TIMING

Dealing Range & Strong / Weak High-Low

Not every swing point deserves equal weight — a Strong High/Low has never been touched since it formed; a Weak one has already been swept and is expected to give way easily.

This is the range Premium/Discount should actually be measured from: the most recent Strong High and Strong Low, which persist across many bars — sometimes weeks — rather than resetting with every minor pullback. A small pullback high protected by a much older, still-untouched high isn't the meaningful resistance; the old high is.

This is also why a Premium/Discount zone can look “too wide” relative to recent price action — it's correctly reflecting a range that simply hasn't been challenged yet, not a bug in how it's drawn.

  • Strong High/Low: unbroken since it formed — still resting, undisturbed liquidity
  • Weak High/Low: already swept once (wicked, not closed through) — lower conviction going forward
  • The Dealing Range = the current Strong High-to-Strong Low span

14SYNTHESIS

Confluence — Reading a Setup

No single concept above is a signal by itself. A real setup is where several of them stack on the same zone — that stacking is what "confluence" actually means in SMC.

Read top to bottom: bias first, then wait for liquidity to be taken, then require structure to confirm the reversal before caring about any zone at all. Skipping steps — acting on a zone without a sweep, or a sweep without structure confirmation — is the most common way this methodology gets misapplied.
